\begin{abstract}
MQTT gateways face two threats at once: present-day network attacks that flood or probe the broker, and harvest-now-decrypt-later (HNDL) adversaries that record cloud-bound traffic for future quantum decryption. Recent work addresses these threats separately: intrusion detection systems are evaluated offline on datasets, while post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is benchmarked as isolated primitives, TLS handshakes, or hardware accelerators. We design, implement, and measure a two-stage gateway on Raspberry Pi hardware. Stage~1 intercepts MQTT-bound flows with Linux NetfilterQueue, extracts 32 flow features that can be reproduced from live packets, and classifies them with a LightGBM model trained on CICIoMT2024 and calibrated on local benign traffic (21 classes; 71.5\% offline accuracy, 0.709 macro-F1). Stage~2 re-encrypts messages that reach the gateway with ML-KEM-768, AES-256-GCM, and ML-DSA-65 (NIST Level~3) without device firmware changes; the envelope is constructed on the gateway and is not transmitted to a cloud receiver. In a labelled two-Pi testbed with five run types, each repeated three times, Stage~1 flagged 1.7\% of benign verdicts and reached precision 1.00 and recall 0.96 on attack traffic; connection, SYN, slow-connection, and scan attacks were flagged in every run, whereas low-rate UDP and publish floods were largely missed. Inline blocking dropped about 3,900 attack packets per run after a mean of 7.3 admitted packets per attack connection, but also cut off long-lived benign connections: 88.6\% of benign messages reached Stage~2 with the gateway's own clients allow-listed, against 59.2\% (34--100\% per run) without. Stage~1 needs a median 2.4~ms of processing per verdict and Stage~2 a median 1.9~ms of cryptographic work per message. Across ten KEM/signature configurations, ML-KEM pairings with ML-DSA or Falcon required 36--53\% less cryptographic time than ECDH+ECDSA for 5~KB payloads, while per-message key regeneration dominates handler latency for Falcon and SLH-DSA.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{M}{QTT}~\cite{mqtt_oasis} is the dominant publish--subscribe protocol for IoT telemetry. Its broker concentrates risk: a single gateway host receives traffic from many constrained devices and forwards it to cloud services. Two classes of adversary target this point. The first acts \emph{now}, flooding the broker with connections or publications, scanning it, or injecting malformed packets. The second acts \emph{later}: a harvest-now-decrypt-later (HNDL) adversary records cloud-bound traffic today and decrypts it once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer breaks the classical key exchange~\cite{shor1994,mosca2018}.

Recent work treats these adversaries separately. Machine-learning intrusion detection systems (IDS) for IoT report accuracies above 99\% on public datasets such as CICIoMT2024~\cite{ciciomt2024,abid2025gwo,wang2025skd,soomro2025roche}, but they are evaluated offline, are not placed in the packet path of a broker, and leave the payload exposed to HNDL collection. Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) studies benchmark the NIST standards ML-KEM~\cite{nist_fips203} and ML-DSA~\cite{nist_fips204} inside TLS~1.3 on a Raspberry Pi~\cite{shahid2026pqtls} or in dedicated ASIC hardware~\cite{dimatteo2026kem22}, but they assume an attack-free network path and do not address MQTT broker admission. No published system classifies live MQTT-bound traffic inline at the broker \emph{and} upgrades the admitted traffic to post-quantum protection, measured jointly on commodity gateway hardware.

This article closes that gap with a threat model, a full description of both stages, an offline and labelled live evaluation of the IDS/IPS, a ten-scheme PQC comparison, and a joint analysis of how the two stages compose. All measurements cover the device-to-gateway path and gateway processing; the cloud receiver is assumed and not evaluated. The main contributions of this article are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textit{Two-Stage Gateway Design:} A Raspberry Pi gateway that classifies MQTT-bound TCP flows in the kernel packet path (NetfilterQueue + LightGBM, Stage~1) and re-encrypts messages that reach Stage~2 with ML-KEM-768 + AES-256-GCM + ML-DSA-65, constructing a protected envelope for an assumed cloud receiver (Section~\ref{sec:design}).
  \item \textit{Offline and Labelled Live IDS/IPS Evaluation:} Per-class results on 484,255 held-out CICIoMT2024 flows with a feature set that is reproducible from live packets, and a labelled two-Pi evaluation (five run types, three repetitions each) that reports per-source flag rates, precision and recall, Stage~1 latency, attack packets admitted and dropped, and benign delivery with and without an infrastructure allow-list (Sections~\ref{sec:method}, \ref{sec:res-labelled}, \ref{sec:res-ids}, and~\ref{sec:res-e2e}).
  \item \textit{PQC Cost on the Gateway:} Per-operation re-encryption latency on 11,661 live messages and a ten-configuration comparison (seven PQC pairings, three classical baselines, three payload sizes, 18,000 messages), including the hidden cost of per-message key regeneration (Sections~\ref{sec:res-pqc} and~\ref{sec:res-matrix}).
  \item \textit{Composition and Deployment Lessons:} The live failure of a model that used all dataset features, the collateral blocking of long-lived benign connections by a per-verdict IPS, the loss of Stage~2 input when the IPS blocks the gateway's own subscriber, and guidance on scheme choice, key caching, and bandwidth (Sections~\ref{sec:method}, \ref{sec:res-e2e}, and~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

The rest of this article is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:threat} states the threat model. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the system, Section~\ref{sec:method} the evaluation methodology, and Section~\ref{sec:results} the results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the article.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Machine-Learning Intrusion Detection for IoT}

Tree ensembles and lightweight deep models dominate recent IoT IDS work because flow features are low-dimensional and inference must be cheap~\cite{iot_ids_survey}. MQTTset~\cite{mqtt_ids_ml} established an MQTT-specific benchmark. CICIoMT2024~\cite{ciciomt2024} captures 18 attacks across MQTT, Wi-Fi, and Bluetooth in an IoMT testbed and reports offline baselines. Abid et al.~\cite{abid2025gwo} combine grey-wolf feature selection with a CNN-enhanced LightGBM and report near-perfect accuracy on CICIoT2023 and CICIoMT2024. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025skd} compress an IDS with self-knowledge distillation and report over 99\% accuracy at reduced model size. Soomro et al.~\cite{soomro2025roche} train a federated IIoT IDS with homomorphic protection of model updates. All four evaluate on stored datasets with accuracy, F1, and confusion matrices; none is placed in the packet path of a live broker, reports per-flow verdict latency on gateway hardware, or protects the payload after admission.

\subsection{Post-Quantum Cryptography for IoT}

Surveys identify lattice-based schemes as the best fit for IoT~\cite{pqc_iot_survey}, and pqm4 showed that ML-KEM's predecessor runs in under a millisecond on Cortex-M4 microcontrollers~\cite{kyber_arm}. Malina et al.~\cite{malina2024mqttpqc} propose quantum-resistant MQTT communication. Shahid et al.~\cite{shahid2026pqtls} integrate ML-KEM and ML-DSA into TLS~1.3 with X.509 certificates on a Raspberry Pi~4 and report handshake latency and certificate overhead. Di~Matteo et al.~\cite{dimatteo2026kem22} build a unified ML-KEM accelerator on 22-nm ASIC and report area, power, and energy. These works measure primitives, handshakes, or silicon; they do not integrate with an IDS, do not upgrade legacy device traffic at the application layer, and evaluate a single scheme family.

\section{Threat Model and Design Goals}
\label{sec:threat}

\textit{System:} Constrained IoT devices publish to a broker on a gateway host; we assume the gateway forwards data to a cloud receiver over a wide-area uplink. The evaluation covers the device-to-gateway path and gateway processing; the cloud receiver is not part of it. Devices are legacy: their firmware and device-side cryptography cannot be changed.

\textit{Adversaries:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{A1---Local network attacker:} Reaches the broker port and launches connection floods, publish floods, SYN/UDP/ICMP floods, scans, and malformed MQTT packets to exhaust or probe the broker.
  \item \textit{A2---HNDL observer:} Passively records gateway-to-cloud traffic and stores it until quantum key recovery becomes feasible.
  \item \textit{A3---Active uplink attacker:} Modifies, replays, or forges messages on the gateway-to-cloud link.
\end{itemize}
The gateway itself is trusted; compromise of the gateway host, side channels, and adversarial evasion of the classifier are out of scope and discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\textit{Design Goals:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{G1:} Limit the broker resources consumed by A1 traffic by classifying flows inline and blocking client connections flagged as attacks.
  \item \textit{G2:} Give cloud-bound data confidentiality against A2 at NIST Level~3 (a design goal of the Stage~2 envelope; cloud reception is not evaluated).
  \item \textit{G3:} Give cloud-bound data integrity and origin authentication against A3, and reject replays (likewise a design goal, requiring a receiver that verifies the signature and tracks $seq$).
  \item \textit{G4:} Require no device firmware changes.
  \item \textit{G5:} Keep per-message overhead small relative to typical publish intervals (seconds).
\end{itemize}

\section{System Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Overview}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the pipeline. All TCP traffic to the broker port enters a NetfilterQueue (NFQUEUE) handled by Stage~1. Packets reach the Mosquitto broker unless their client connection has already been blocked. Stage~2 subscribes to device topics, re-encrypts each message that it receives, and constructs a protected envelope for an assumed cloud receiver; in the prototype the envelope is not published (Section~\ref{sec:keys}). A message reaches Stage~2 only if the gateway's subscriber remains connected, so Stage~1 admission to the broker is not the same as Stage~2 processing.

\subsection{Stage 1: LightGBM Flow Classifier and IPS}
\label{sec:stage1}

\textit{Interception:} An \texttt{iptables} rule sends packets for the broker port to NFQUEUE. A user-space callback parses IP/TCP (and UDP) headers with \texttt{dpkt}. To keep classification off the critical path, each packet is \emph{accepted back to the kernel before} the classifier runs; inference time therefore delays only later packets waiting in the queue, not the current one.

\textit{Flow State:} A bidirectional flow table keyed by the 5-tuple stores, for every packet, its frame length, IP TTL, TCP flags, header length, direction, and arrival time. A flow is classified 1)~when it terminates with FIN or RST after at least 3 packets; 2)~every 20 packets, so that long-lived connections such as MQTT sessions and publish floods receive one verdict per 20 packets; and 3)~by a background reaper when idle for 10~s. Flows that end or expire with fewer than 3 packets are logged as unclassified.

\textit{Features:} Each verdict uses 32 CICIoMT2024 features computed over the packets collected so far: packet rate (three columns); the share of packets carrying each TCP flag (FIN, SYN, RST, PSH, ACK, ECE, CWR); 15 protocol indicators (application protocol by port, transport, and link layer); and the minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation, and total of the frame lengths, with the derived Magnitude and Radius. We matched each column to the semantics of the released data, which differ from their names in places: lengths include the 14-byte Ethernet header, and flag columns are shares rather than counts. The other 13 dataset columns are not used because a live flow cannot reproduce them. Eleven depend on how the dataset was windowed during capture (header length, flag counts, total sum, IAT, Number, Covariance, Variance, and Weight), and two mix packets from different flows (Duration, which tracks the IP TTL in the released data, and Protocol Type).

\textit{Classifier:} Features are standardized and passed to a 21-class LightGBM model~\cite{lightgbm2017} (benign plus 20 attack classes). A flow is labelled an attack when the arg-max class is not benign and the benign probability is below $1-\tau$, with $\tau = 0.5$.

\textit{Prevention:} In IPS mode, an attack verdict adds the client side of the flow (client IP, protocol, client port, broker port) to a blocklist; later packets matching it are dropped in the callback, and an \texttt{iptables} drop rule for the same tuple is inserted. Normalizing to the client side avoids blocking the broker's own replies from port 1883. Because the key includes the client port, a new connection from the same host is not covered by an earlier block. Blocks do not expire. Source addresses on an optional allow-list are exempt: their attack verdicts are logged but never blocked. In IDS mode, verdicts are logged and nothing is dropped.

\textit{Enforcement Timing:} Three intervals separate a flow's first packet from its first dropped packet: 1)~\emph{collection time}, until the flow reaches a classification trigger (minimum packet count with FIN/RST, every $N$ packets, or 10~s of idleness); 2)~\emph{classification processing time}, for feature extraction, inference, and decision; and 3)~\emph{enforcement delay}, from the verdict to the next packet of the blocked tuple. Every packet before the verdict is admitted to the broker. Stage~1 therefore cuts off the remainder of long-lived attack connections, such as publish floods, but cannot stop connections that finish before a verdict: flows shorter than the minimum packet count are never classified, and a flow classified on FIN/RST has already ended, so its block affects only that tuple. Short connect floods and scans can thus complete before any blocking decision.

\subsection{Stage 2: Post-Quantum Re-Encryption}
\label{sec:stage2}

For every message received on a device topic, Stage~2 executes Algorithm~\ref{alg:reenc}. If the device used an encrypted scheme, the gateway first recovers the plaintext with the device key. It then encapsulates a fresh ML-KEM-768 shared secret to the cloud public key, encrypts the plaintext with AES-256-GCM under that secret and a random 96-bit nonce, and signs the encoded envelope with the gateway's ML-DSA-65 key.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Stage 2 Re-Encryption of an Admitted Message $m$}
\label{alg:reenc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $p \gets \textsc{DeviceDecrypt}(m)$ \Comment{identity if plaintext}
\State $(c_\mathrm{kem}, ss) \gets \textsc{ML-KEM-768.Encaps}(pk_\mathrm{cloud})$
\State $\eta \xleftarrow{\$} \{0,1\}^{96}$
\State $c_\mathrm{aes} \gets \textsc{AES-256-GCM}_{ss}(p, \eta)$
\State $M \gets \textsc{Encode}(id, seq, c_\mathrm{kem}, \eta, c_\mathrm{aes})$
\State $\sigma \gets \textsc{ML-DSA-65.Sign}(sk_\mathrm{gw}, M)$
\State \textbf{return} $\{id, seq, c_\mathrm{kem}, \eta, c_\mathrm{aes}, \sigma\}$ \Comment{envelope for the cloud}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textit{Signed Encoding:} $\textsc{Encode}$ outputs a fixed domain tag followed by the five fields $id$ (UTF-8), $seq$ (unsigned 64-bit, big-endian), $c_\mathrm{kem}$, $\eta$, and $c_\mathrm{aes}$, each prefixed by its 4-byte big-endian length. Length prefixes make the encoding unambiguous: two different field tuples can never produce the same signed bytes. The Stage~2 timings in Section~\ref{sec:res-pqc} were recorded with this encoding.

\textit{Security Properties:} By construction, a fresh encapsulation per message gives each message an independent key, so compromise of one message key does not expose others, and the IND-CCA2 security of ML-KEM-768 protects the uplink against A2 at NIST Level~3. ML-DSA-65 (EUF-CMA) over the encoded envelope gives integrity and gateway origin authentication against A3, and binding the signature to $(id, seq)$ allows a cloud receiver that tracks the last accepted $seq$ per device to reject replays. Signing the ciphertext rather than the plaintext also prevents an observer from confirming guesses of low-entropy sensor readings against a public signature. Cloud-side signature verification, decryption, and replay rejection are expected protocol properties, not experimentally demonstrated results (Section~\ref{sec:keys}). The device-to-gateway link keeps whatever scheme the device already uses (AES-256 under Grover retains 128-bit security), which meets G4 but does not add forward secrecy on that segment.

\subsection{Key Provisioning and Experimental Assumptions}
\label{sec:keys}

\textit{Provisioning:} Before the experiments, a single 256-bit master secret, written as 64 hexadecimal characters, is placed out of band in a local environment file on the experimental nodes. Each process loads it at start-up; it is never sent over MQTT or any other network channel.

\textit{Derivation:} Every key is derived from the master secret with HKDF-SHA256~\cite{rfc5869} (no salt) under a domain-separated context string that names the algorithm and, where applicable, the device, e.g., \texttt{aes:}$\langle id\rangle$, \texttt{ecdsa-p256:}$\langle id\rangle$, \texttt{kem:}$\langle alg\rangle$\texttt{:keypair}, and \texttt{sig:}$\langle alg\rangle$\texttt{:}$\langle id\rangle$\texttt{:keypair}. AES-256 and ChaCha20-Poly1305 keys are taken directly from HKDF output, and P-256 private keys are HKDF output reduced modulo the group order. PQC key pairs are produced by the standard liboqs key-generation routines while the liboqs random-number source is temporarily replaced, through its custom-RNG interface (\texttt{OQS\_randombytes\_custom\_algorithm}), by a callback that returns a 64-byte HKDF output repeated cyclically; after key generation, including on error, the system RNG is restored (\texttt{OQS\_randombytes\_switch\_algorithm}). The hook requires the bindings to use the same liboqs shared library; all real-liboqs PQC messages in the scheme comparison were accepted, which requires matching key pairs on client and gateway. Every node therefore reproduces the same key pairs without a key exchange. KEM key pairs are scoped per algorithm, so all devices encrypt to one gateway KEM key per algorithm; signature key pairs are scoped per device. Encapsulation randomness and AES-GCM nonces come from the system RNG.

\textit{Purpose and Measurement:} This setup makes runs reproducible and keeps key distribution out of the measured path: provisioning happens before any measurement and is not part of any reported latency. The scheme-comparison gateway is stateless and rederives the gateway KEM key pair and the sending device's verification key for every message. This regeneration falls inside the total handler time but outside the decapsulation, decryption, and verification timers, and Section~\ref{sec:res-matrix} reports the resulting non-cryptographic time separately. Stage~2 likewise derives its keys inside the handler, before its encapsulation timer starts.

\textit{Cloud Receiver:} The cloud receiver is an assumed deployment component; it is not implemented or evaluated. Stage~2 constructs the protected envelope at the gateway, and we measure gateway-side encapsulation, encryption, and signing; in the prototype the envelope is not transmitted, so delivery, decapsulation, decryption, signature verification, and replay rejection at a cloud receiver are not evaluated. As a stand-in for the cloud public key, Stage~2 encapsulates to the ML-KEM-768 key pair derived from the master secret and signs with an ML-DSA-65 key derived for a fixed relay identity. In a deployment, the cloud receiver would generate and hold its own ML-KEM private key, and the gateway would use an authenticated copy of the corresponding public key together with its own signing key.

\textit{Trust Assumptions:} The experiments assume a single trusted administrative domain. In the live sessions, the broker, both stages, and the gateway's own subscriber run on one Raspberry Pi, the benign clients and attack generators run on a second Raspberry Pi on the same Wi-Fi network, and all nodes are controlled by the researchers. The master secret is assumed to be unavailable to attackers; the attack generators do not use it or any derived key. The secret is not rotated during the reported experiments. These are experimental assumptions, not security guarantees.

\textit{Limits of a Shared Secret:} A shared master secret is a single point of failure: whoever obtains it can derive every key derived from it, including device keys, the gateway's KEM private key, and the stand-in cloud key. Deterministic shared-seed key generation is a controlled-testbed convenience, not a recommended production provisioning method. A production deployment requires independently provisioned device keys, private keys protected in hardware (e.g., a secure element or TPM), separate cloud key management in which the gateway never holds the cloud private key, and key rotation and revocation. The reported encapsulation, decapsulation, signing, and verification times measure operations on keys already in memory and do not depend on how those keys were generated. Production key management (enrolment, certificate validation, hardware key access, rotation, and revocation) would add overhead that we do not measure.

\subsection{Implementation}

Both stages run on a Raspberry Pi~4B (BCM2711, four Cortex-A72 cores at 1.8~GHz, 4~GB LPDDR4) under Python~3.11; in the live sessions, benign clients and attack generators run on a second Raspberry Pi~4B, and both Pis join the same Wi-Fi access point (a mobile hotspot). Stage~1 uses \texttt{NetfilterQueue}, \texttt{dpkt}, and LightGBM; Stage~2 uses \texttt{liboqs-python}~\cite{oqs2023}, the \texttt{cryptography} package for AES-GCM, and Paho-MQTT with Mosquitto. Timestamps come from \texttt{time.perf\_counter\_ns()}, and records are written by a background thread to avoid perturbing measurements.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Model Training and Calibration}
\label{sec:training}

\textit{Training:} The LightGBM model is trained on the CICIoMT2024 Wi-Fi/MQTT flows with the 32 features of Section~\ref{sec:stage1}. A stratified 70/30 split gives 1,129,927 training and 484,255 test flows (multiclass objective, learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, early stopping on 10\% of the training split; training time 14.0~s). We report per-class precision, recall, F1, accuracy, and macro-F1 on the test split.

\textit{Pilot and Calibration:} A pilot benign-only session (C1, 10.6~min) on the live testbed, run with an earlier model that used all 45 dataset features, showed severe dataset shift. That model labelled 96.4\% of the verdicts on benign client connections (242 of 251) and all 125 verdicts on the gateway's own subscriber as attacks. We therefore restricted the model to the reproducible features and added the 375 classified benign flow segments of C1, each replicated 20 times (7,500 rows), to the training split only; the test split is unchanged. C1 is not used in any reported live result. Because C1 used the same benign generators as the evaluation, the live false-positive rates describe a deployment that is calibrated on its own benign traffic, not an uncalibrated model.

\subsection{Live Workloads}

\textit{Benign Clients:} Four generators model legitimate devices: a \emph{steady} sensor publishing every 2~s; a \emph{mixed} device with random 1--3~s intervals and occasional idle periods; a \emph{burst} device that sends 3--8 messages at 5~msg/s and then idles for 5--15~s; and a monitoring \emph{subscriber} to all device topics (QoS~1). Each publisher embeds a device identifier and a monotonic sequence number in every message and logs every publish with its result code. Stage~2's own subscriber runs on the gateway host and is treated as infrastructure.

\textit{Attack Clients:} Ten generators reproduce CICIoMT2024 MQTT and TCP/IP attack families against the broker port, each at a low rate of one message, connection, or packet per second per thread: MQTT publish flood, malformed MQTT packets, slow-connection (Slowloris-style) exhaustion holding 5 connections, MQTT connect flood (serial and with 5 parallel threads), port scan (ports 1870--1900), vulnerability scan, UDP flood (1 and 5 threads), and TCP SYN flood. ICMP floods are not evaluated because the NFQUEUE rule matches only broker-port traffic, and replay is excluded.

\subsection{Live Testbed, Runs, and Metrics}
\label{sec:protocol}

\textit{Testbed:} The gateway Pi runs Stage~1, the Mosquitto broker, and Stage~2; a second Pi runs all benign clients and attack generators, so live traffic crosses a Wi-Fi link. A control agent on the gateway starts every session from a clean state (broker restarted, block rules cleared) with identical Stage~1 settings (Section~\ref{sec:stage1}, threshold $\tau = 0.5$), and a runner on the client Pi then starts the workload.

\textit{Runs:} Five run types are each repeated three times (15 sessions), and we report the mean $\pm$ standard deviation over repetitions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{R1:} Benign clients only for 10~min, IDS mode.
  \item \textit{R2:} The ten attacks in sequence, each for 60~s with 20~s gaps, without benign clients, IDS mode.
  \item \textit{R3:} The R2 attack sequence with benign clients running throughout, IDS mode.
  \item \textit{R4:} As R3 in IPS mode, with the loopback address allow-listed so that the gateway's own subscriber cannot be blocked.
  \item \textit{R5:} As R4 without the allow-list.
\end{itemize}
R2--R5 sessions last 14.2~min each.

\textit{Ground Truth:} The client Pi logs the local port of every benign connection, the start and end of every attack window, and every benign publish; the clock offset between the Pis is measured at session start (at most 38~ms). Each flow seen by Stage~1 is labelled from these logs: flows from the loopback or gateway address are \emph{infrastructure}; flows from the client Pi on a logged benign port are \emph{benign}, attributed to their client; and other client-Pi flows are attributed to the attack whose window, extended by the flow timeout plus 5~s, contains the first packet. The remaining client-Pi flows (about 48 per IPS session, single packets just outside the connect-flood window, never classified) are left unlabelled.

\textit{Metrics:} Because a long-lived connection receives one verdict per 20 packets, detection is reported per verdict: the \emph{flag rate} of a source is the share of its verdicts that are attacks, and precision, recall, and F1 are computed over attack and benign-client verdicts, with infrastructure reported separately. For IPS runs, the \emph{benign delivery ratio} is the share of successful benign publishes whose (device, sequence number) reached Stage~2; \emph{attack packets before verdict} is the number of packets of a blocked attack connection admitted before its first attack verdict; \emph{attack packets dropped} sums the \texttt{iptables} block-rule counters and in-callback drops; and blocked benign and infrastructure connections are counted from block events.

\subsection{PQC Measurements}

\textit{Stage~2 on Live Traffic:} We time KEM encapsulation, AES-GCM encryption, signing, their sum, and the full handler (MQTT callback to completion) for every message received by the gateway subscriber in R1 and R3--R5. These timers are gateway-side processing costs; they are not added to Stage~1 classification time to form an end-to-end latency, and they do not include broker transit or cloud delivery. The live benign clients publish unencrypted JSON, so $\textsc{DeviceDecrypt}$ is the identity and these messages carry no device-side PQC; Stage~2 alone applies post-quantum protection, at the gateway. The constructed envelope is logged and not forwarded.

\textit{Scheme Comparison:} This separate benchmark evaluates device-to-gateway PQC: clients encrypt padded payloads of 5,120, 10,240, and 102,400 bytes under ten configurations (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}), 600 messages per configuration and size (18,000 messages, all accepted), and the gateway times decapsulation, decryption, and verification. Security level $L$ is the minimum NIST level of the KEM and signature. The configurations were measured sequentially in one session each, without interleaving or repeated sessions. PQC operations use liboqs; P-256 ECDH and ECDSA use OpenSSL through Python's \texttt{cryptography} package. AES-256-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305 are symmetric-only reference configurations: they perform no public-key establishment or signature verification and are not security-equivalent alternatives to the asymmetric configurations.

\textit{Timer Boundaries:} The crypto timer is the sum of the key-establishment, decryption, and verification timers; Base64 decoding, JSON handling, and logging fall outside it but inside the total handler time. The timers are not perfectly equivalent across libraries: the ECDH timer includes parsing the ephemeral public point and the HKDF derivation of the AES key, whereas the PQC decapsulation timer covers liboqs decapsulation without equivalent Base64 parsing or a KDF. The ECDH+ECDSA handler caches its static ECDH key and the device verification keys, whereas the PQC handler regenerates its key pairs for every message outside the crypto timer.

\subsection{Offline Classifier Performance}
\label{sec:res-offline}

On the held-out set the model reaches 71.5\% accuracy, 0.709 macro-F1, and 0.706 weighted F1 (Fig.~\ref{fig:offline}). Restricting the model to live-reproducible features costs offline accuracy: the earlier 45-feature model reached 97.85\% accuracy and 0.900 macro-F1 on a held-out set of the same size but failed on live traffic (Section~\ref{sec:training}). Benign traffic is still recognized with 0.955 precision and 0.972 recall, so 2.8\% of benign test flows are assigned to an attack class. SYN floods (F1 0.96--0.98), MQTT connect floods (0.88--0.99), port scans (0.947), and TCP DDoS (0.903) remain well separated, and publish floods reach 0.83--0.85 and malformed MQTT 0.719. The loss concentrates in classes that the excluded windowing and timing columns used to separate: UDP floods (0.30--0.64), ICMP floods (0.55--0.68), TCP DoS (0.705), and low-volume reconnaissance such as OS scan (0.656), vulnerability scan (0.306), and ping sweep (0.216, only 56 test flows).

\subsection{Inline Blocking}
\label{sec:res-e2e}

Table~\ref{tab:ips-effect} compares the two IPS runs. Without blocking, every successful benign publish reached Stage~2 in all R1 and R3 runs, so any loss in R4 and R5 is caused by blocking.

\textit{Attack Suppression:} In both IPS runs every labelled attack connection received at least one verdict. Blocked attack connections were admitted 7.3 packets on average before their verdict, after which the block rules dropped about 3,900 attack packets per session. Flag rates of the connect floods (about 80\%) and slow connections (about 42\%) are lower than in R3 because blocked clients retry, and their retransmissions and reconnections form additional short segments, some classified as benign. The UDP floods remained unflagged and unblocked.

\textit{Collateral Blocking:} With the allow-list (R4), the gateway subscriber drew an attack verdict 11--13 times per session, and the allow-list prevented every one of these blocks. Benign clients had no exemption: 13.0$\pm$2.6 benign connections were blocked per session, each after a single attack verdict. Their clients reconnected on new ports (14.3 benign connections per session, against 4 in R3), but messages published while a connection was blocked were lost, and 88.6$\pm$5.3\% of benign messages reached Stage~2 (82.6, 91.9, and 91.5\% in the three runs). Without the allow-list (R5), the outcome depended on whether the gateway subscriber drew an attack verdict. In the first repetition it did not, and delivery was 100\%. In the other two, its connection was blocked 5 and 6 times at intervals of about 2~min, each time after it had reconnected, and delivery fell to 43.8\% and 33.8\%. A gateway that hosts its own MQTT clients behind an IPS must therefore exempt them, and per-verdict blocking of long-lived benign connections needs a further safeguard, such as requiring repeated attack verdicts or expiring blocks, which we have not evaluated.

\textit{Message Size:} Even AES-only expands a 5,120-byte payload to 7,314~B (+43\%) because of Base64 encoding and JSON metadata. Post-quantum material enlarges the 5~KB message by 1.27$\times$ (Falcon-512) to 4.22$\times$ (FrodoKEM + SLH-DSA) over AES-only, but only by 1.4--17\% at 100~KB, which favours batching small readings before protection.

\textit{Key Regeneration:} The PQC benchmark handler is stateless: it rederives the gateway KEM key pair and the device verification key for each message. The resulting non-cryptographic handler time, which includes key regeneration together with decoding, JSON handling, and logging, is 1.3--1.6~ms for ML-KEM/ML-DSA, 22~ms for Falcon-512, 19~ms for FrodoKEM, 45~ms for BIKE, and 155--174~ms for SLH-DSA, about 90$\times$ the cryptographic work. Practical gateways should cache keys per device and algorithm. Caching is expected to remove most of this overhead and to make Falcon and SLH-DSA viable, but a key-cached PQC handler was not measured.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the Two Stages Compose}

The stages address different adversaries at different layers, but simultaneous operation is not the same as experimentally validated end-to-end security or availability. Stage~1 acts in the packet path ahead of the broker, where attack connections can be cut off cheaply from flow statistics once classified, although packets before the verdict (7.3 per blocked attack connection in our runs) reach the broker. Stage~2 acts on messages that the gateway subscriber receives, constructing a post-quantum envelope; it does not, in this prototype, transmit that envelope, and it processes a message only while that subscriber remains connected. The coupling between the stages is therefore not only the admitted traffic: a false verdict on the gateway subscriber removes Stage~2's input altogether, as R5 showed. PQC work is therefore spent only on traffic that reaches Stage~2, not on packets that Stage~1 later drops, and long-term confidentiality of a transmitted envelope would not depend on the classifier being correct. The two stages also run as separate processes (packet callback vs.\ MQTT callback): Stage~1 inference can delay later packets waiting in the queue, but the Stage~1 and Stage~2 timers are not added to form an end-to-end latency.

\subsection{Deployment Guidance}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Scheme:} ML-KEM-768 + ML-DSA-65 gives NIST Level~3 for under 0.8~ms of gateway cryptographic time at 5~KB; its advantage over ECDH+ECDSA narrows with payload size, and PQC key caching would be needed for it to carry over to total handler time, which we have not measured; ML-KEM-512 + Falcon-512 is the most bandwidth-efficient Level~1 option; FrodoKEM and BIKE suit only latency-tolerant links.
  \item \textit{Keys:} Cache key pairs per device and algorithm; per-message derivation dominates cost for Falcon and SLH-DSA.
  \item \textit{Bandwidth:} The fixed PQC overhead is large for small messages; batch readings where latency allows.
  \item \textit{Features:} Use only features that the live extractor can reproduce exactly; a model that used all 45 dataset features labelled 96\% of benign verdicts as attacks in our pilot.
  \item \textit{IPS Policy:} Exempt infrastructure clients (the gateway's own subscriber and monitoring clients) from blocking, start in IDS mode, calibrate on local benign traffic, and allow-list registered devices before enabling blocking. A per-verdict policy blocks a long-lived benign connection after its first false verdict, so a low per-verdict false-positive rate (1.7\%) still cost 11\% of benign messages in R4.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Testbed:} All benign and attack traffic came from one client Pi over one Wi-Fi network, so many-device contention, multiple attacker addresses, and per-address blocking are not evaluated.
  \item \textit{Attack Coverage:} Attacks ran at low rates (one event per second per thread); low-rate UDP and publish floods were largely missed, higher-rate variants were not tested, and ICMP floods and replay were excluded.
  \item \textit{Ground Truth:} Labels come from client-side logs and attack time windows, not from per-packet tags; flows near window boundaries can be mislabelled, and about 48 single-packet flows per IPS session stayed unlabelled.
  \item \textit{Calibration:} The model was calibrated on a pilot session that used the same benign generators as the evaluation, so the live false-positive rates are optimistic for unseen benign devices; the 32-feature model also loses offline accuracy (71.5\% vs 97.85\%) relative to the 45-feature model.
  \item \textit{Timing Conditions:} The live Stage~1 and Stage~2 timings are about 3 and 12 times higher than in an earlier single-host session; CPU frequency, governor, and throttling state were not recorded, and the cause is not established.
  \item \textit{Enforcement Delay:} We measure packets admitted before a verdict and packets dropped afterwards, but not the time from a verdict to the first enforced drop.
  \item \textit{Evasion:} An adversary who knows the features can shape traffic to look benign; adversarial robustness is not evaluated.
  \item \textit{Scheme Benchmark:} Configurations ran sequentially without independent repetitions, timer boundaries are implementation-specific (Section~\ref{sec:method}), and the PQC and classical handlers use different key-caching policies.
  \item \textit{Runtime:} Python adds dispatch and parsing overhead that a C implementation would avoid; absolute latencies are therefore conservative.
  \item \textit{Joint Evaluation:} The IPS runs measure benign delivery to Stage~2 under attack, not cloud delivery; HNDL confidentiality and uplink authentication (G2, G3) are properties of the constructed envelope, not experimentally demonstrated against a cloud observer or active attacker.
  \item \textit{Cloud Receiver:} The cloud receiver is assumed, not implemented; its cost is only a reference from gateway receive-side measurements, and WAN latency is not included (Section~\ref{sec:keys}).
  \item \textit{Not Measured:} End-to-end message latency, energy, WAN transit time, and multi-gateway scaling.
  \item \textit{Key Management:} All keys derive from one shared master secret under the trust assumptions of Section~\ref{sec:keys}; this is a testbed convenience and a single point of failure, not a production provisioning scheme.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a two-stage MQTT gateway that classifies live MQTT-bound flows with a LightGBM model in the kernel packet path, blocks client connections flagged as attacks, and constructs an ML-KEM-768 + AES-256-GCM + ML-DSA-65 envelope on messages that reach Stage~2, and evaluated it on a labelled two-Pi testbed. Restricting the model to features that a live flow can reproduce, and calibrating it on local benign traffic, brought the per-verdict benign flag rate from 96\% to 1.7\%; connection, SYN, slow-connection, and scan attacks were flagged in every run, but low-rate UDP and publish floods were largely missed. Stage~1 needs a median 2.4~ms of processing per verdict and Stage~2 a median 1.9~ms of cryptographic work per message; these are separate gateway costs, not a measured end-to-end latency. Inline blocking admitted about 7 packets per attack connection before cutting it off, but per-verdict blocking also cut off long-lived benign connections: 88.6\% of benign messages reached Stage~2 with the gateway's own clients allow-listed, and as few as 34\% without, when the IPS blocked Stage~2's own subscriber. Across ten configurations, ML-KEM pairings with ML-DSA or Falcon needed less cryptographic time than ECDH+ECDSA at 5~KB, an advantage that narrows at 100~KB and that the per-message PQC key regeneration outweighed in total handler time; key caching is therefore expected, though not yet measured, to decide both this comparison and whether Falcon and SLH-DSA are practical. Future work will evaluate blocking policies that tolerate isolated false verdicts, higher-rate floods, energy, many-device deployments, adversarially robust detection, and a native implementation.
