\begin{abstract}
Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) gateways face attacks that flood or probe the broker and harvest-now-decrypt-later collection of the traffic they forward. Intrusion detectors are usually scored on stored datasets, and post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is usually timed as a primitive or a Transport Layer Security (TLS) handshake. This article measures both on one Raspberry Pi gateway. Stage~1 classifies MQTT-bound Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) flows in the kernel packet path with LightGBM and blocks a connection after two consecutive attack verdicts. An attack prediction counts only when the client sent at least 30\% of the flow segment's payload bytes. Stage~2 re-encrypts each admitted message with Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism (ML-KEM-768), Advanced Encryption Standard in Galois/Counter Mode (AES-256-GCM), and Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (ML-DSA-65) and posts the envelope over Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure (HTTPS). The receiver verifies the signature, decrypts, and rejects replays. Devices keep their existing cryptography. On a three-Pi local-area network (LAN), with benign publishers on two addresses and attacks from one or both, benign delivery was 0.997--1.000 and detection F1 was 0.986--0.995. A dual-benign session produced 1,315 accepted envelopes, with a median verify-and-decrypt time of 0.209~ms. Over the three-Pi sessions, median classification took 2.61~ms and median re-encryption 1.47~ms. In intrusion-prevention-system (IPS) mode, 76.3\% (E3, two repetitions) and 78.8\% (E5, three repetitions) of attack connections were blocked, and no benign connection was blocked. At 5~KB, ML-KEM with ML-DSA or Falcon needs 36--53\% less cryptographic time than elliptic-curve Diffie--Hellman with the elliptic-curve digital signature algorithm (ECDH+ECDSA), which places ML-KEM-768 + ML-DSA-65 inside a few milliseconds at National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Level~3.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{M}{essage} Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT)~\cite{mqtt_oasis} is the dominant publish--subscribe protocol for Internet of Things (IoT) telemetry. Its broker concentrates risk: a single gateway host receives traffic from many constrained devices and forwards it to cloud services. Two classes of adversary target this point. The first acts \emph{now}, flooding the broker with connections or publications, scanning it, or injecting malformed packets. The second acts \emph{later}: a harvest-now-decrypt-later (HNDL) adversary records cloud-bound traffic today and decrypts it once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer breaks the classical key exchange~\cite{shor1994,mosca2018}.

These two problems are still measured apart. Machine-learning intrusion detection systems (IDS) for IoT report accuracies above 99\% on stored traces such as CICIoMT2024~\cite{ciciomt2024,abid2025gwo,wang2025skd,soomro2025roche}, without a verdict in the packet path of a live broker and without protecting the payload after admission. Post-quantum studies time ML-KEM~\cite{nist_fips203} and ML-DSA~\cite{nist_fips204} as TLS~1.3 handshakes on a Raspberry Pi~\cite{shahid2026pqtls} or as ASIC kernels~\cite{dimatteo2026kem22}, on an attack-free path that does not decide which MQTT clients to admit. None of the works in Section~\ref{sec:related} classifies live MQTT-bound traffic inline and upgrades the admitted messages to post-quantum protection, with both costs measured on the same gateway.

This article does that. A Raspberry Pi gateway classifies MQTT-bound TCP flows in the kernel packet path and re-encrypts the messages it admits. The operating scheme is ML-KEM-768 + AES-256-GCM + ML-DSA-65. A background post delivers each envelope over HTTPS to a receiver that verifies, decrypts, and rejects replays. After the first use, the abbreviations in the list above are used without expansion. The contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textit{Gateway:} Inline LightGBM intrusion prevention (NetfilterQueue) composed with post-quantum re-encryption, so legacy devices keep their current cryptography and only admitted messages are upgraded (Section~\ref{sec:design}).
  \item \textit{Live evaluation:} A three-Pi LAN with benign publishers on two addresses, attacks from one or both, and an HTTPS receiver, with connection-level blocking and per-verdict latency measured on those runs (Sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item \textit{Scheme choice:} A ten-configuration comparison on the gateway. At 5~KB, ML-KEM with ML-DSA or Falcon is faster than ECDH+ECDSA, and ML-KEM-768 + ML-DSA-65 is the Level~3 point that stays within a few milliseconds, which is why it is the scheme used live (Section~\ref{sec:res-matrix}).
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:threat} states the threat model and Section~\ref{sec:design} the design. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the evaluation. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports, in order, the offline classifier, the scheme comparison that selects ML-KEM-768 + ML-DSA-65, the three-Pi IDS/IPS results, and the cloud receiver. Section~\ref{sec:limits} states the limits, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Machine-Learning Intrusion Detection for IoT}

Flow features are low-dimensional, so recent IoT intrusion detectors favour tree ensembles and small neural nets that are cheap to evaluate~\cite{iot_ids_survey}. MQTTset~\cite{mqtt_ids_ml} made that evaluation MQTT-specific. CICIoMT2024~\cite{ciciomt2024} captures 18 attacks over MQTT, Wi-Fi, and Bluetooth in an IoMT testbed and publishes offline baselines. Later models push accuracy on stored traces: Abid et al.~\cite{abid2025gwo} combine grey-wolf feature selection with a CNN-enhanced LightGBM and report near-perfect accuracy on CICIoT2023 and CICIoMT2024; Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025skd} compress an IDS by self-knowledge distillation and report over 99\% accuracy at a smaller model; Soomro et al.~\cite{soomro2025roche} train a federated IIoT IDS and protect the model updates with homomorphic encryption. Each of these papers answers a dataset question (accuracy, model size, or federated overhead). None places the classifier in the packet path of a live broker, reports the time to a verdict on gateway hardware, or protects the payload after a flow is admitted.

\subsection{Post-Quantum Cryptography for IoT}

Surveys single out lattice schemes as the practical fit for IoT~\cite{pqc_iot_survey}, and pqm4 showed that ML-KEM's predecessor finishes in under a millisecond on a Cortex-M4~\cite{kyber_arm}. Malina et al.~\cite{malina2024mqttpqc} argue for quantum-resistant MQTT, which is the right protocol target, but the argument stops at the cryptographic construction. The closest implementations are elsewhere. Shahid et al.~\cite{shahid2026pqtls} put ML-KEM and ML-DSA into TLS~1.3 with X.509 certificates on a Raspberry Pi~4 and measure handshake latency and certificate size. Di~Matteo et al.~\cite{dimatteo2026kem22} implement ML-KEM as a 22-nm accelerator and report area, power, and energy. Both show that the NIST schemes can be fast. Both assume the path is already safe to use: there is no decision about which MQTT clients to admit, legacy devices must be changed or are out of scope, and only one scheme family is timed.

\subsection{Positioning}

Table~\ref{tab:related} puts the six closest works on the same axes as this gateway. The point of the table is not a higher accuracy. It is that the axes are usually measured one at a time.

Dataset IDS papers set the accuracy bar. Dadkhah et al., Abid et al., Wang et al., and Soomro et al.\ separate IoT attacks in stored flows, in several cases above 99\%. Those figures are not the right comparison for a live broker. They score a frozen feature file. This paper scores verdicts, blocks, admitted attack packets, and benign delivery while attacks are aimed at Mosquitto. Our offline macro-F1 on the 32 features that live packets can reproduce is lower than models that use the full dataset vector, and we do not claim otherwise. The claim those papers do not make is that a verdict is enforced in the kernel path, that a false positive has a measured cost, and that an admitted message is then re-encrypted.

On the cryptographic side, Shahid et al.\ are the nearest deployment: the same Pi class, and the same ML-KEM and ML-DSA families, inside TLS. Every endpoint in that design must speak the new handshake, and the measurement assumes the path is free of broker floods. Di~Matteo et al.\ show a lower bound on what ML-KEM can cost in silicon. They do not sit behind an IDS, and they do not carry MQTT. Malina et al.\ name the protocol this paper actually serves, without an inline prevention policy or a measured choice among parameter sets.

This paper is the only row that is live on the MQTT path, inline as an IPS, and post-quantum on the messages the gateway admits, with the receiver included in the measurement. The comparison that justifies the operating point is the ten-configuration timing in Section~\ref{sec:res-matrix}: at a 5~KB payload, ML-KEM with ML-DSA or Falcon needs less cryptographic time than ECDH+ECDSA, and ML-KEM-768 + ML-DSA-65 is the NIST Level~3 pair that remains inside a few milliseconds on the Pi. That is a gateway result, not a leaderboard result. It does not improve on ASIC energy, federated training, or offline accuracy, and it does not try to.

\section{Threat Model and Design Goals}
\label{sec:threat}

\textit{System:} Constrained IoT devices publish to a broker on a gateway host. The gateway admits or blocks client connections and forwards admitted messages over HTTPS to a cloud receiver. The receiver in this evaluation is a PC on the same LAN, not a wide-area uplink. Devices are legacy: their firmware and device-side cryptography are not changed.

\textit{Adversaries:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{A1, local network attacker:} Reaches the broker port and launches connection floods, publish floods, SYN/UDP/ICMP floods, scans, and malformed MQTT packets to exhaust or probe the broker.
  \item \textit{A2, HNDL observer:} Passively records gateway-to-cloud traffic and stores it until quantum key recovery becomes feasible.
  \item \textit{A3, active uplink attacker:} Modifies, replays, or forges messages on the gateway-to-cloud link.
\end{itemize}
The gateway host is trusted. Compromise of that host, side channels, and adversarial evasion of the classifier are out of scope (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\textit{Design Goals:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{G1:} Limit the broker resources consumed by A1 by classifying flows inline and blocking client connections that the policy marks as attacks.
  \item \textit{G2:} Confidentiality of cloud-bound payloads against A2 at NIST Level~3, by encapsulating a fresh AES-256-GCM key with ML-KEM-768. This holds only if the cloud private key is generated and stored securely (Section~\ref{sec:keys}). The evaluation shows that the receiver decapsulates and decrypts accepted envelopes. It does not record the uplink and attempt a later break, so it is not an HNDL attack experiment.
  \item \textit{G3:} Integrity and gateway origin authentication against A3, by an ML-DSA-65 signature over the encoded envelope, plus rejection of a sequence number that is not strictly newer than the last accepted value for that device. The evaluation runs that check. It does not include an active forgery campaign against the uplink.
  \item \textit{G4:} No device firmware changes. Devices keep the cryptography they already use.
  \item \textit{G5:} Per-message overhead small relative to typical publish intervals, which are on the order of seconds.
\end{itemize}

\section{System Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Overview}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the pipeline. All TCP and UDP traffic to and from the broker port enters a NetfilterQueue handled by Stage~1. Packets reach the Mosquitto broker unless that client connection has already been blocked. Stage~2 subscribes to device topics, re-encrypts each message it receives, and posts the envelope over HTTPS. The MQTT callback does not wait for the HTTP response. A message reaches Stage~2 only while the gateway's subscriber stays connected, so admission to the broker is not the same as re-encryption.

\subsection{Stage 1: LightGBM Flow Classifier and IPS}
\label{sec:stage1}

\textit{Interception:} An \texttt{iptables} rule sends packets for the broker port to NFQUEUE. A user-space callback parses IP/TCP (and UDP) headers with \texttt{dpkt}. To keep classification off the critical path, each packet is \emph{accepted back to the kernel before} the classifier runs; inference time therefore delays only later packets waiting in the queue, not the current one.

\textit{Flow State:} A bidirectional flow table keyed by the 5-tuple stores, for every packet, its frame length, IP TTL, TCP flags, header length, direction, and arrival time. A flow is classified 1)~when it terminates with FIN or RST after at least 3 packets; 2)~every 20 packets, so that long-lived connections such as MQTT sessions and publish floods receive one verdict per 20 packets; and 3)~by a background reaper when idle for 10~s. Flows that end or expire with fewer than 3 packets are logged as unclassified.

\textit{Features:} Each verdict uses 32 CICIoMT2024 features computed over the packets collected so far: packet rate (three columns); the share of packets carrying each TCP flag (FIN, SYN, RST, PSH, ACK, ECE, CWR); 15 protocol indicators (application protocol by port, transport, and link layer); and the minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation, and total of the frame lengths, with the derived Magnitude and Radius. We matched each column to the semantics of the released data, which differ from their names in places: lengths include the 14-byte Ethernet header, and flag columns are shares rather than counts. The other 13 dataset columns are not used because a live flow cannot reproduce them. Eleven depend on how the dataset was windowed during capture (header length, flag counts, total sum, IAT, Number, Covariance, Variance, and Weight), and two mix packets from different flows (Duration, which tracks the IP TTL in the released data, and Protocol Type).

\textit{Classifier:} Features are standardized and passed to a 21-class LightGBM model~\cite{lightgbm2017} (benign plus 20 attack classes). The model predicts an attack when the arg-max class is not benign and the benign probability is below $1-\tau$, with $\tau = 0.5$.

\textit{Client-Share Rule:} The broker forwards every published payload to the subscribers of its topic, so the flow of a subscriber carries the attack traffic that it receives. An attack prediction is therefore kept only if the client sent at least a share $\gamma = 0.3$ of the segment's payload bytes (of its packets, if the segment carries no payload); otherwise the verdict is benign. Payload bytes are used rather than packets because a QoS~1 subscriber acknowledges every forwarded message and so sends about half of the packets but few of the bytes. The model's raw prediction is logged with every verdict.

\textit{Prevention:} In IPS mode, a client connection is blocked after $K = 2$ consecutive attack verdicts; a benign verdict resets the count. A block adds the client side of the flow (client IP, protocol, client port, broker port) to a blocklist; later packets matching it are dropped in the callback, and an \texttt{iptables} drop rule for the same tuple is inserted. Packets from the broker to a blocked connection are dropped as well and are no longer classified. Normalizing to the client side avoids blocking the broker's replies to other clients. Because the key includes the client port, a new connection from the same host is not covered by an earlier block. Blocks do not expire. Source addresses on an optional allow-list are exempt: their attack verdicts are logged but never blocked. In IDS mode, verdicts are logged and nothing is dropped.

\textit{Enforcement Timing:} Three intervals separate a flow's first packet from its first dropped packet: 1)~\emph{collection time}, until the flow has reached a classification trigger (minimum packet count with FIN/RST, every $N$ packets, or 10~s of idleness) for the $K$-th consecutive time; 2)~\emph{classification processing time}, for feature extraction, inference, and decision; and 3)~\emph{enforcement delay}, from the blocking verdict to the next packet of the blocked tuple. Every packet before the blocking verdict is admitted to the broker. Stage~1 therefore cuts off the remainder of long-lived attack connections, such as publish floods, but cannot stop connections that finish before a second verdict: flows shorter than the minimum packet count are never classified, and a connection that receives only one verdict, such as a SYN-flood or connect-flood attempt classified on FIN/RST or a slow connection classified once when idle, is detected but never blocked. For connections that have already ended, a block would cover only a finished tuple.

\subsection{Stage 2: Post-Quantum Re-Encryption}
\label{sec:stage2}

For every message received on a device topic, Stage~2 executes Algorithm~\ref{alg:reenc}. If the device used an encrypted scheme, the gateway first recovers the plaintext with the device key. It then encapsulates a fresh ML-KEM-768 shared secret to the cloud public key, encrypts the plaintext with AES-256-GCM under that secret and a random 96-bit nonce, and signs the encoded envelope with the gateway's ML-DSA-65 key.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Stage 2 Re-Encryption of a Message $m$ Received by Stage 2}
\label{alg:reenc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State $p \gets \textsc{DeviceDecrypt}(m)$ \Comment{identity if plaintext}
\State $(c_\mathrm{kem}, ss) \gets \textsc{ML-KEM-768.Encaps}(pk_\mathrm{cloud})$
\State $\eta \xleftarrow{\$} \{0,1\}^{96}$
\State $c_\mathrm{aes} \gets \textsc{AES-256-GCM}_{ss}(p, \eta)$
\State $M \gets \textsc{Encode}(id, seq, c_\mathrm{kem}, \eta, c_\mathrm{aes})$
\State $\sigma \gets \textsc{ML-DSA-65.Sign}(sk_\mathrm{gw}, M)$
\State \textbf{return} $\{id, seq, c_\mathrm{kem}, \eta, c_\mathrm{aes}, \sigma\}$ \Comment{envelope for the cloud}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textit{Signed Encoding:} $\textsc{Encode}$ outputs a fixed domain tag followed by the five fields $id$ (UTF-8), $seq$ (unsigned 64-bit, big-endian), $c_\mathrm{kem}$, $\eta$, and $c_\mathrm{aes}$, each prefixed by its 4-byte big-endian length. Length prefixes make the encoding unambiguous: two different field tuples can never produce the same signed bytes. The Stage~2 timings in Section~\ref{sec:res-pqc} were recorded with this encoding.

\textit{Security Properties:} A fresh encapsulation gives each message its own key, so compromise of one message key does not expose the others. IND-CCA2 security of ML-KEM-768 is what G2 relies on, and EUF-CMA security of ML-DSA-65 over the encoded envelope is what G3 relies on. Binding the signature to $(id, seq)$ is what lets the receiver reject replays. Signing the ciphertext, rather than the plaintext, stops an observer from testing guesses of a low-entropy sensor reading against a public signature. These are properties of the construction. They hold in a deployment only with authentic provisioning of the cloud public key and the gateway verification key (Section~\ref{sec:keys}). The device-to-gateway hop is unchanged, which meets G4 and adds no forward secrecy on that hop. AES-256 on that hop retains 128-bit security against Grover's algorithm.

\textit{Cloud Receiver:} A background thread posts each envelope over HTTPS. The receiver holds the ML-KEM-768 private key, checks the ML-DSA-65 signature against a pinned gateway key, decapsulates, decrypts, and accepts the message only when its sequence number is strictly newer than the last accepted value for that device. An equal sequence number is a duplicate. An older one is a replay and is rejected. The receiver in this evaluation is on the LAN. The uplink is not subjected to an active forgery or to an HNDL experiment, which is the limit already stated in G2 and G3.

\subsection{Keys}
\label{sec:keys}

Device and gateway keys used on the MQTT hop are derived from one 256-bit master secret, placed out of band and never sent on the network. HKDF-SHA256~\cite{rfc5869} with a context string that names the algorithm and the device lets every node reproduce the same key without a handshake, which keeps key distribution out of the measured path. For reproducibility, PQC key generation draws from a deterministic SHAKE-256 stream in place of the liboqs random-number generator; encapsulation nonces and AES-GCM nonces come from the system generator. The cloud KEM private key is created on the receiver, and the gateway signing key is created on the gateway. Each private key stays on the machine that created it.

That master secret is a testbed convenience and a single point of failure: anyone who obtains it can derive every device key that comes from it. The NIST levels in this article name the parameter sets, not a production key hierarchy. Reported cryptographic times are operations on keys already in memory. Enrolment, hardware-backed private keys, rotation, and revocation are not in the measurement.

\subsection{Implementation}

The gateway is a Raspberry Pi~4B (BCM2711, four Cortex-A72 cores at 1.8~GHz, 4~GB LPDDR4) running Python~3.11, Mosquitto, Stage~1 (\texttt{NetfilterQueue}, \texttt{dpkt}, LightGBM), and Stage~2 (\texttt{liboqs-python}~\cite{oqs2023}, AES-GCM, Paho-MQTT). Two further Raspberry Pi~4B boards are the clients. A PC on the same Wi-Fi LAN is the HTTPS receiver. Timestamps use \texttt{time.perf\_counter\_ns()}, and logs are written on a background thread so that disk I/O stays off the timed path.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Classifier}
\label{sec:training}

The Stage~1 model is a 21-class LightGBM classifier on the 32 features of Section~\ref{sec:stage1}. Training uses the CICIoMT2024 Wi-Fi and MQTT rows together with local benign flow segments from the same generators used in the live runs. Those local segments are not part of the reported sessions. Offline metrics use a stratified 70/30 split of the dataset's released test partition: 1,129,927 training rows and 484,255 held-out rows. The split is by row, so rows from one capture can fall on both sides. The test split is held out from training. It is not an independent capture.

\subsection{Workloads}

Four benign generators run on the client Pis: a steady publisher every 2~s, a mixed publisher at 1--3~s with idle gaps, a burst publisher (3--8 messages at 5~msg/s, then 5--15~s idle), and a QoS~1 subscriber to all device topics. Each publisher carries a device identifier and a monotonic sequence number. The gateway's own subscriber is infrastructure.

Ten attack generators reproduce CICIoMT2024 families against the broker port: MQTT publish flood, malformed MQTT, slow connection (five held sockets), connect flood (serial and five threads), port scan, vulnerability scan, UDP flood (one and five threads), and TCP SYN flood. The publish flood sends 40 messages/s, inside the dataset's median rate for that class. The UDP floods send 500 datagrams/s, far below the dataset median of about 21,000--27,000 packets/s, because the Python NFQUEUE path cannot absorb that rate. The other generators send one event per second per thread. ICMP floods and MQTT replay are outside the live runs: ICMP has no broker port, and replay needs a recorded benign capture.

\subsection{Three-Pi Runs}
\label{sec:protocol}

The gateway Pi runs Stage~1, Mosquitto, and Stage~2. Two client Pis source the workload. A PC on the same Wi-Fi LAN is the HTTPS receiver. Each session starts from a restarted broker and an empty blocklist, with $\tau = 0.5$, $\gamma = 0.3$, and $K = 2$.

Five run types are each repeated three times. Means are over those repetitions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{E1:} Both client Pis publish only benign traffic, for 10~min, in IPS mode. Benign client addresses are allow-listed.
  \item \textit{E2:} One client Pi stays benign. The other runs all ten attacks, each for 60~s with 20~s gaps. IDS mode.
  \item \textit{E3:} The same traffic as E2, in IPS mode. The benign client address is allow-listed. The attacking address is not.
  \item \textit{E4:} The ten attacks are split into two disjoint halves, one per client Pi, with benign publishers still running. IDS mode.
  \item \textit{E5:} The same split as E4, in IPS mode. Client addresses are not allow-listed. The gateway's loopback subscriber is.
\end{itemize}
E3 is reported over two repetitions. The third did not complete and is excluded. Connection-level block rates, Stage~1 and Stage~2 latencies, and recall with the UDP verdicts removed are computed from these sessions.

Each client logs benign ports, attack-window start and end, and every benign publish. The clock offset between Pis is measured at session start. A flow from the gateway address is infrastructure. A flow from a logged benign port is benign. Any other client flow is attributed to the attack window that contains its first packet, extended by the flow timeout plus 5~s.

Detection is scored per verdict. A long-lived flow is classified every 20 packets, so a busy source contributes more verdicts than a short one. Precision and recall use attack and benign-client verdicts. Infrastructure is reported separately. Where the supporting tables break recall down by family, macro-averaged recall is the unweighted mean of the ten family flag rates, so a flood with thousands of verdicts does not dominate a scan with one.

Blocking is scored separately from detection. An attack connection is blocked when a block event is recorded for its client tuple. Benign delivery is the share of successful publishes, return code 0, whose device and sequence number reach Stage~2. Packets before block are the packets of a blocked attack connection up to the verdict that blocked it. Attack packets dropped are the packets matched by a block rule or dropped in the callback for an attack tuple.

\subsection{Cryptographic Measurements}

Gateway cryptographic time is the sum of encapsulation or decapsulation, AES-GCM, and the signature operation. It is not added to the Stage~1 verdict time, and it does not include Wi-Fi transit or the HTTPS round trip. On the live path the devices publish plaintext JSON, so Stage~2 is the only post-quantum step. Receiver time is signature check, decapsulation, and decryption on accepted envelopes.

The scheme comparison times ten configurations at payloads of 5,120, 10,240, and 102,400 bytes, 600 messages each, all accepted by the gateway. Security level is the minimum NIST level of the KEM and the signature. ML-KEM, ML-DSA, Falcon, BIKE, FrodoKEM, and SLH-DSA use liboqs. ECDH and ECDSA use P-256 through OpenSSL. AES-256-GCM and ChaCha20-Poly1305 are symmetric references, not security-equivalent alternatives. The PQC handler regenerates its key pairs on every message, outside the cryptographic timer. The ECDH handler caches its static keys. That difference is why handler time and cryptographic time are reported separately, and why the choice of ML-KEM-768 + ML-DSA-65 is argued from cryptographic time at 5~KB.

\subsection{Offline Accuracy}
\label{sec:res-offline}

On the held-out set the 32-feature model reaches 71.5\% accuracy and 0.709 macro-F1 (Fig.~\ref{fig:offline}). Benign flows are recognized at 0.955 precision and 0.972 recall. Connection-oriented attacks stay separated: SYN floods at F1 0.96--0.98, MQTT connect floods at 0.88--0.99, port scans at 0.947. The loss sits in classes that the excluded windowing and timing columns used to separate, including UDP floods (F1 0.30--0.64) and vulnerability scan (0.306). A model on all 45 dataset columns reaches 97.85\% accuracy on a held-out set of the same size. Those extra columns cannot be computed from a live flow. Frame-length spread and TCP flag shares account for most of the split gain (37.5\% and 29.2\%), so the live model separates traffic by packet-size profile and flag mix.

\subsection{Scheme Comparison and Selection}
\label{sec:res-matrix}
\label{sec:res-pqc}

Table~\ref{tab:matrix} is the reason ML-KEM-768 + ML-DSA-65 is the scheme on the live path. At a 5~KB payload, ML-KEM paired with ML-DSA or Falcon needs 0.56--0.77~ms of cryptographic time, 36--53\% less than ECDH+ECDSA at 1.21~ms. The gap is the key establishment: ML-KEM decapsulation takes 0.13--0.19~ms, against 0.62~ms for the P-256 ECDH timer.

ML-KEM-768 + ML-DSA-65 is the NIST Level~3 point in that set. Its cryptographic time is 0.772~ms and its handler, which regenerates the PQC key pair on every message, is 2.32~ms. ML-KEM-512 + ML-DSA-44 is faster (0.562~ms) and only Level~1. Falcon-512 matches the cryptographic time (0.572~ms) and produces the smallest post-quantum message (9,253~B, against 13,216~B for the Level~3 pair), but the handler takes 22.5~ms because the key pair is rebuilt per message. SLH-DSA, FrodoKEM, and BIKE miss a realtime budget: cryptographic time from 1.72~ms to 36.6~ms, and handler time from 81~ms to 193~ms.

At 100~KB the same ML-KEM pairings need 3.14--3.50~ms against 3.73~ms for ECDH+ECDSA, and ML-KEM-768 + ML-DSA-65 costs 1.9$\times$ an AES-256-GCM-only reference. The 10~KB payloads fall between these two. The live gateway therefore uses ML-KEM-768 + ML-DSA-65: Level~3, under a millisecond of cryptography at 5~KB, and a handler of a few milliseconds on the Pi. A cached key would be required before Falcon or SLH-DSA could be reconsidered. A key-cached PQC handler was not measured. AES-only already expands a 5,120-byte payload to 7,314~B; the Level~3 envelope is 13,216~B.

\subsection{Three-Pi IDS and IPS Evaluation}
\label{sec:res-three-pi}

Table~\ref{tab:three-pi} is the security result. Benign publishers run on two addresses, and attacks come from one address or from both.

With both clients benign (E1), the flag rate on benign verdicts was 3.0$\pm$0.7\% across eight long-lived connections, no benign connection was blocked, and 0.997$\pm$0.001 of successful publishes reached Stage~2. There were no attacks, so precision and recall are undefined. When one client ran all ten attacks, IDS mode (E2) gave precision 0.999, recall 0.991, and F1 0.995. That recall is dominated by the UDP floods, which produce about 2,890 of 4,098 attack verdicts (70.5\%) and are flagged on every verdict. With those verdicts removed, recall is 0.969. Macro-averaged recall over the ten families is 0.986. The same traffic in IPS mode (E3, two repetitions) gave precision 0.997$\pm$0.003, recall 0.976$\pm$0.001, and F1 0.986$\pm$0.002, with benign delivery 1.000 and no benign connection blocked. Blocking cuts the UDP floods after two verdicts, so they leave only 12 of 738 attack verdicts (1.6\%), and recall without them is 0.975. Splitting the attack list across the two clients gave F1 0.995$\pm$0.001 in IDS mode (E4: recall 0.991, 0.970 without UDP, macro 0.986) and 0.989$\pm$0.005 in IPS mode (E5: recall 0.982, 0.981 without UDP).

Table~\ref{tab:ips-block} is the IPS result. E3 blocked 76.3\% of labelled attack connections over two completed repetitions, and E5 blocked 78.8\% over three. Together that is 1,629 of 2,092 connections (77.9\%). No benign connection was blocked. Publish floods, malformed MQTT, and the UDP floods were blocked in every completed IPS run. Connect floods were blocked at 91--98\% (serial) and 89--97\% (five threads); the leftovers had already closed. SYN floods and the vulnerability scans received one attack verdict and had already finished, so a block would have named a dead socket. The port scan was blocked in one of the five runs and had finished in the others. All five slow-connection sockets stayed open until the attack window ended, because they never received two consecutive attack verdicts. About 12 packets were admitted before a block, and about 57,700 (E3) and 57,650 (E5) attack packets were then dropped per repetition. E2 and E4 log verdicts and do not block.

The UDP-flood share of recall is a property of the mode: IDS sessions keep those floods alive, and IPS sessions cut them off. An earlier two-Pi study of the same policy, four repetitions and one client address, blocked 80.6\% and 80.2\% of attack connections. Those figures are not E1--E5 measurements. Without the client-share rule, that earlier mixed-traffic set would have scored precision 0.930, because subscribers that receive forwarded attack payloads were flagged on 54\% and 61\% of their raw predictions.

\subsection{Cloud Verification, Delivery, and Latency}
\label{sec:res-cloud}

The receiver is off the MQTT callback. In the dual-benign session used for cloud delivery it accepted 1,315 envelopes, 902 from one client Pi and 413 from the other. The receiver checked the ML-DSA-65 signature, decapsulated with ML-KEM-768, and decrypted with AES-256-GCM. The median of those three operations was 0.209~ms. An older sequence number was rejected as a replay. Sessions in which the receiver was not accepting are not reported as cloud-delivery results. This is LAN delivery of the envelope. It is not a wide-area latency, and it is not an active attack on the uplink.

Across the 14 analyzed three-Pi sessions, Stage~1 recorded 39,244 verdicts at a median 2.61~ms (P95 4.69~ms). Stage~2 recorded 15,891 messages at a median re-encryption time of 1.47~ms and a median full-handler time of 1.65~ms. These are separate processing costs. They are not added to the 0.209~ms receiver time, and they do not include Wi-Fi transit or the HTTPS round trip. Against publish intervals of seconds they meet G5. The uplink grows by a fixed 4,425~B per message (ML-KEM ciphertext, ML-DSA signature, nonce, and GCM tag), paid on the gateway-to-cloud hop, not by the device. An earlier two-Pi study timed Stage~1 at 2.41~ms and Stage~2 re-encryption at 2.07~ms. Those figures are not E1--E5 measurements.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Stage~1 and Stage~2 answer different adversaries. Stage~1 spends 2.61~ms to decide a flow and then drops the rest of a long-lived attack, after about 12 packets have already reached the broker. Stage~2 spends 1.47~ms of cryptography only on a message the subscriber actually received, and the receiver accepts that envelope in 0.209~ms on the LAN (Section~\ref{sec:res-cloud}). The two timers are not an end-to-end latency: they run in different processes, and the HTTPS post does not block the MQTT callback. Confidentiality of the envelope does not depend on the classifier being right. A missed attack is still re-encrypted if the subscriber receives it. A blocked flood never reaches Stage~2.

The client-share rule is what keeps that subscriber alive. The broker copies published payloads to every subscriber, so a flow classifier sees the attack on the subscriber's connection. Without the rule, those raw predictions score precision 0.930. With it, mixed-traffic precision is 0.999, and the gateway subscriber is not blocked. Blocking that subscriber would remove Stage~2's input.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\textit{Two-verdict policy.} A block requires two consecutive attack verdicts because a benign MQTT session is itself long-lived and is classified every 20 packets. The first false attack verdict is not grounds to cut that session off. On earlier two-Pi mixed-traffic verdicts, blocking after one attack verdict would have blocked 2.5 benign connections and the gateway subscriber per session, against 0.25 with $K = 2$. In the three-Pi IPS runs, $K = 2$ blocked no benign connection. The same rule is why the leftover 22\% of attack connections in E3 and E5 were not cut off: they never received two attack verdicts in a row.

\textit{Unblocked slow connections.} Publish floods, malformed MQTT, and the UDP floods produce a verdict every 20 packets, so they are cut off in every IPS run. A SYN flood or a leftover connect-flood attempt is classified once, on FIN or RST, and the socket is already gone. A slow-connection socket holds a broker connection and sends too little to reach a second 20-packet verdict. It is classified once, by the idle reaper, and left open for the rest of the attack window. All five of those sockets behaved this way in every completed E3 and E5 run. Detecting them is not the same as releasing the broker state they hold. A policy that blocked on one idle verdict, or on hold time, would cover them and would give up the protection $K = 2$ gives benign sessions.

\textit{Incomplete E3 repetition.} One E3 repetition stopped early (14 of 184 benign publishes reached Stage~2) and is excluded. E3 means are over the other two repetitions.

\textit{Cloud logging.} Cloud delivery is reported for the dual-benign session in which the receiver was accepting. Later sessions that re-encrypted on the gateway but recorded no new accepts are not used as cloud-delivery results. The 0.209~ms figure is signature check, decapsulation, and decryption on the LAN, not a wide-area round trip.

\textit{Keys.} Device keys on the MQTT hop come from one shared master secret so that G4 holds and no handshake enters the timed path (Section~\ref{sec:keys}). That secret is a single point of failure for those device keys. The cloud KEM private key and the gateway signing key are generated separately. The NIST level names the parameter sets, not a production enrolment scheme.

\textit{What was not measured.} Energy is not reported because the claim is processing time against a publish interval of seconds. A joules-per-message figure would depend on the CPU governor, the Wi-Fi radio between publishes, and interpreter overhead, none of which was held fixed. The UDP floods run at 500 datagrams/s rather than the dataset median of about 21,000--27,000 packets/s because the Python NFQUEUE path cannot absorb that rate and still classify. ICMP floods and MQTT replay are outside the runs. An attacker who coaxes the broker into sending most of the payload bytes can fall below the client-share threshold. The testbed is three Pis and one PC on one Wi-Fi LAN. Offline accuracy uses a row split of one dataset partition. The scheme timings are one pass per configuration, with PQC keys regenerated and classical static keys cached. Adversarial inputs shaped to look benign were not evaluated.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A Raspberry Pi MQTT gateway can classify live flows in the packet path and re-encrypt the messages it admits. With benign publishers on two client addresses and attacks from one or both, benign delivery stayed at 0.997--1.000 and detection F1 at 0.986--0.995. The LAN receiver accepted 1,315 envelopes in the dual-benign session, with a median verify-and-decrypt time of 0.209~ms. Under the two-verdict policy, E3 blocked 76.3\% of attack connections and E5 blocked 78.8\%, after about 12 packets, including every long-lived flood, at a median 2.61~ms per verdict and 1.47~ms of re-encryption. No benign connection was blocked. The connections left open had been detected once: SYN floods and short scans had already closed, and the five slow-connection sockets were still holding broker state. An earlier two-Pi study blocked 80.6\% and 80.2\% at 2.41~ms and 2.07~ms; those figures are not part of E1--E5. ML-KEM-768 + ML-DSA-65 is the scheme used on that path because, at 5~KB, it is the NIST Level~3 pair that stays under a millisecond of cryptography and a few milliseconds of handler time on the Pi. What remains is a control for single-verdict sockets, UDP floods at dataset rate, device keys that are not derived from one shared secret, and energy and wide-area delay measured under conditions that those numbers would actually transfer.
